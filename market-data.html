Rare Earth Metal Prices, Supply, and Production

America mined a record tonnage of rare earths in 2025 and got paid less for it. In this market, that sentence is not a contradiction.

Production: more tons, fewer dollars

The United States mined more rare earths in 2025 than in any year in the USGS series — and earned less for them. The 2026 Mineral Commodity Summaries put 2025 domestic production at an estimated 51,000 metric tons of rare-earth-oxide equivalent, up from 45,000 tons in 2024, valued at $240 million against $260 million the year before. Volume up, value down: the classic signature of a market where prices, not output, set the story. Bastnaesite from Mountain Pass, California, remains the primary domestic source, with monazite from southeastern heavy-mineral sands alongside it.

Prices: the heavy elements diverge

Light rare earths are cheap by the kilogram; heavy rare earths are not, and the gap widened in 2025. USGS average prices for 2025 show terbium oxide at $1,010 per kilogram, up from $812 in 2024, while dysprosium oxide eased to $239 from $257. At the other end of the table, lanthanum oxide averaged about $1.00 per kilogram and cerium oxide $1.71. The lesson for anyone reading a single 'rare earth price' headline: there is no such thing. Seventeen elements, seventeen markets, and the ones that matter for magnets trade at multiples of the ones that matter for catalysts — which is why the leading domestic end use (catalysts) and the leading global end use (magnets) point to different price tables.

SMM notes European heavy rare-earth inventories are shrinking — “prices but no available goods.” SMM’s read on China domestic trade: the October rally was short-lived, and the November outlook is weak. The SMM European spot assessments and China domestic figures above are on different bases from each other and from the USGS annual averages.

One small number carries outsized weight. Samarium oxide sold for an estimated $2.82 per kilogram in 2025, according to the 2026 USGS summaries as cited by the Colorado School of Mines' Payne Institute — yet a USGS disruption model ranked samarium the mineral whose one-year supply loss would cost U.S. GDP the most, nearly $4.5 billion in 2023 dollars, because of its role in high-temperature samarium-cobalt magnets. Price and strategic importance, in this market, barely correlate.
