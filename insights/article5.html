The November 10 Clock: What Happens When China’s Rare Earth Suspension Expires

critical metals investing dysprosium terbium neodymium · November 2026 · 6 min read

On a Tuesday morning in early November 2026, a procurement manager at a Midwestern magnet maker opened the Chinese Ministry of Commerce website and searched for a document that did not exist. For a year, her company had operated under a suspension: Beijing's October 2025 package of rare earth export controls, including the rule that would have required a Chinese export licence for any foreign-made product containing more than 0.1% Chinese-origin rare earth by value, had been paused as part of a US-China trade truce. The pause expires on November 10. As of the first week of November, no extension had been published. No replacement text. Nothing.

She was not the only one checking. Across defense, automotive, and chip fabrication, supply chain managers were reading the same silence. The April 2025 controls on seven heavy rare earths were never suspended, and they have already remade the market. The October 2025 package, if it reactivates, reaches further: it would put a licence requirement on products made outside China, in Vietnam, Thailand, South Korea, and Japan, wherever Chinese feedstock is inside them.

Two prices for the same kilogram

The gap the controls created is now the most striking number in commodity markets. The China Rare Earth Industry Association's benchmark index read 258.3 on October 28, a figure that suggests mild consolidation. It says almost nothing about what material actually costs outside China. Dysprosium oxide traded domestically in China at roughly $210 to $216 per kilogram in late October. Outside China, Rare Earth Exchanges assessed dysprosium oxide at $2,500 to $5,500 per kilogram. Terbium tells the same story in sharper relief: about $951 per kilogram in China on November 5, according to Shanghai Metals Market data, against $7,300 to $8,300 per kilogram in Europe the same week. Samarium, the cheapest of the heavies, trades at $23 to $38 per kilogram in China and has been quoted at $2,000 to $2,200 per kilogram delivered in North America.

These are not same-day, same-grade pairs, and honest analysts say so: the ex-China figures are indicative quotes from thin markets, not liquid exchange prices. But the direction and the scale are not in dispute. Benchmark Mineral Intelligence found CIF Europe dysprosium prices rose about 150% over the third quarter of 2026 alone, reaching roughly $3,500 per kilogram by September. Fastmarkets assessments put dysprosium at $270 to $330 per kilogram FOB China against $1,250 to $1,675 per kilogram in Rotterdam. The premium is the price of a licence, a delay, and a risk.

The physical flows confirm what the prices show. China shipped a record 6,165 tonnes of magnets in September, up 23% month on month, which looked like recovery until the cumulative figure was read: January-to-September rare earth exports were down 9.6% year on year at 43,711 tonnes. The September spike was a backlog clearing after July-August tightening, not a new trend. US magnet shipments averaged 504 tonnes a month in 2026, about 19% below the pre-control average. Chinese yttrium oxide exports to Japan fell from 605.3 tonnes in the first five months of 2025 to 14.1 tonnes in the same period of 2026, a 97.7% decline.

The other clock: January 1

November 10 is not the only deadline. On January 1, 2027, the US Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement ban on Chinese-origin rare earth magnets in defense systems takes full effect. Two valves close seven weeks apart: one controlled by Beijing, one by Washington. Between them sits a Western supply base that is real but small. Lynas Rare Earths produced 7,260 tonnes of NdPr in fiscal 2026 and began separating terbium in Malaysia in 2025, the first commercial heavy rare earth separation outside China. MP Materials is annualizing near 4,000 tonnes of NdPr and was commissioning heavy rare earth separation at Mountain Pass in 2026. Energy Fuels produced its first terbium oxide in March 2026. Together, the Western producers supply a fraction of global NdFeB magnet demand, which runs 315,000 to 345,000 tonnes a year. China makes about 94% of the world's sintered permanent magnets and holds roughly 91% of separation and refining capacity.

The policy response has been to put a floor under Western production rather than to cap Chinese prices. The US Department of Defense agreement with MP Materials sets a $110-per-kilogram price floor for NdPr, and Lynas holds a similar arrangement. The floor matters because it changes the investment question: a Western separation plant no longer has to survive a Chinese price war to be financeable. It has to survive construction, permitting, and commissioning. That is still a hard list, but it is a different kind of hard.

The counterargument: the gap is not the market

Skeptics of the panic have a case, and it deserves a hearing. The widest gaps, samarium at 346 times and yttrium at 100 times or more, compare Chinese domestic prices with delivered North American quotes for material that has already cleared the licensing system. They measure scarcity of cleared stock, not the cost of the element. China has also issued general licences to some exporters since late 2025, shortening delays for buyers who qualify. And the record September magnet shipments show that material does move when Beijing allows it. A market that can ship 6,165 tonnes in a month is not a market that has stopped.

There is also the 2011 precedent. The last rare earth crisis ended in a decade-long bear market as new supply arrived and demand adjusted. High prices are already funding the cure: every Western separation project announced in 2026 traces its economics to the gap. If the November 10 suspension is extended, or if Beijing decides that magnet exports are more valuable as revenue than as leverage, ex-China prices could fall faster than Western plants can be built.

But the counterargument has a limit. The April 2025 controls were never suspended, and the October package was suspended for one year, not withdrawn. The structure of the market has changed even if the prices have not settled: buyers now pay for traceability, for stock outside the licensing system, and for supply that does not require a ministry's permission. Those are permanent costs of a bifurcated market, not transitory spikes.

What to watch next

Three things decide the next six months. First, whether a MOFCOM text appears before November 10 extending the suspension, and on what terms. Second, whether the January 1 defense ban accelerates Western magnet orders or simply reroutes them through allied processors. Third, whether Lynas and MP Materials can convert separation capacity into delivered heavy rare earths at volume, because the fund thesis for the sector has quietly shifted from owning mines to owning the chemistry between the mine and the magnet.

The $3,250 kilogram of dysprosium that opened this series is already history. The price that matters now is the one nobody can quote: the cost of a kilogram that has not yet been licensed. Until November 10 answers itself, that is the number every buyer in the market is really paying attention to.
