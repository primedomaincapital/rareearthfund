How Long Does It Take to Bring a New Rare Earth Supply Online?

rare earth supply timeline permitting · November 2026 · 6 min read

In July 2017, MP Materials acquired the Mountain Pass rare earth mine in California out of bankruptcy. The mine had ore in the ground, a processing plant on the surface, and a history that stretched back to the 1950s. It took six years — until 2023 — before Mountain Pass produced its first separated neodymium-praseodymium oxide, the material that goes into the magnets in electric vehicles and wind turbines. Six years to restart a mine that already existed. The question of how long it takes to bring a new rare earth supply online has an answer, and it is measured in decades, not years.

S&P Global's study of 127 mines brought into production since 2002 puts the average discovery-to-production timeline at 15.7 years. In the United States, the average is 29 years. In Australia, it is 20 years. Those are averages across all minerals, and rare earths are slower than the average, because a rare earth mine is not one project but three: a mine, a separation plant, and a magnet supply chain — and the second and third do not exist outside China at any meaningful scale. A company that discovers a rare earth deposit today is, on the historical record, looking at a production date in the 2040s.

The projects that are actually moving

The fastest-moving project in the Western world is Iluka Resources' Eneabba refinery in Western Australia, and it is not fast. The investment decision was taken on April 4, 2022. Commissioning is scheduled for mid-2027 — roughly five years from decision to first production — and the cost has risen from A$1.0–1.2 billion to A$1.7–1.8 billion. Five years, and a 50% cost overrun, for a refinery that processes material already mined. The second project, Lynas Rare Earths' Kalgoorlie cracking and leaching plant, took its first feed in December 2023, on a budget that rose from A$730 million to about A$800 million. These are the success stories. They are also the slowest success stories in the mining industry.

Mountain Pass shows what the ramp looks like once the plant exists. The mine produced 50,692 tonnes of rare earth oxide concentrate in 2025, and 2,599 tonnes of separated NdPr — up from 200 tonnes in 2023. In the first half of 2026, NdPr production reached 1,757 tonnes, up 51% on the same period a year earlier. The trajectory is real, and it is steep. But the scale is the story: 2,599 tonnes of separated NdPr in a year, against a global market measured in tens of thousands of tonnes, and a Chinese industry that produces the overwhelming majority of it. Mountain Pass is the largest rare earth producer outside China, and it supplies a single-digit share of global separated output.

What the government is doing — and what it cannot do

The United States government has decided that the timeline is a national security problem, and it is spending accordingly. In July 2025, the Department of Defense took a direct equity stake in MP Materials, becoming its largest shareholder at roughly 15%, and set a $110-per-kilogram price floor for NdPr for ten years — a guarantee that the mine's output will be worth at least that much, whatever the market does. The Department also provided a $150 million note in August 2025. In February 2022, it committed $35 million to a heavy rare earth separation facility. And from January 1, 2027, a statute requires that magnets in defense systems be free of Chinese-origin content — a deadline that the Pentagon itself has acknowledged the supply chain cannot yet meet, given the F-35's acknowledged reliance on Chinese magnets.

The private sector is moving on the same timeline, from the other direction. Apple's $500 million commitment to recycled rare earth magnets, announced for 2027, is the largest single corporate bet on secondary supply — magnets recovered from old devices, reprocessed, and put back into new ones. MP Materials' Independence plant in Fort Worth, Texas, is designed to produce 3,000 tonnes a year of finished magnets, with General Motors contracted for about 1,000 tonnes, and shipments beginning in the fourth quarter of 2026. The 10X project at Northlake, backed by more than $1.25 billion, is designed for 7,000 tonnes a year, with commissioning in 2028. These are real plants, with real customers, and real dates. They are also, collectively, a fraction of what China produces in a year.

The permitting process is where most of those years go, and it is the part of the timeline that no amount of money can shorten. A new mine in the United States faces a federal environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act, state mining permits, water and air permits, and — for rare earths — a radiological assessment, because rare earth ores are commonly associated with thorium. Each of those processes runs on its own clock, with its own public comment periods and its own litigation exposure, and they run sequentially rather than in parallel more often than the industry would like. Mountain Pass was permitted in the 1950s, when none of this existed; a mine proposed today would spend seven to ten years in permitting before a shovel moved. The federal government's FAST-41 process and the 2025 executive orders on critical minerals permitting are designed to compress that schedule, but as of November 2026 no rare earth mine has yet been permitted through the expedited route. The process is the timeline, and the timeline is the investment.

The counterargument: the timeline is compressing

The case that the timeline is shortening rests on three facts, and they are real. First, the government money is unprecedented — a price floor, an equity stake, and a statutory deadline together are a demand guarantee the industry has never had. Second, the technology is improving: MP Materials went from 200 tonnes of NdPr in 2023 to 2,599 tonnes in 2025, a thirteen-fold increase in two years, which suggests the separation bottleneck is a learning problem rather than a physics problem. Third, recycling is a genuinely new source of supply: Apple's $500 million is not a rounding error, and every magnet recovered from a discarded phone is a magnet that does not need to be mined.

But the counterargument has a limit, and the limit is the S&P Global number. The 15.7-year average, the 29-year American average, and the 20-year Australian average are not forecasts. They are measurements of what has actually happened, across 127 mines, over two decades — and they include the permitting, the financing, the construction, and the ramp-up. A price floor does not shorten a permitting process. An equity stake does not build a separation plant faster. The Iluka and Lynas projects, which are the best-executed rare earth projects in the Western world, took five years from decision to commissioning and still overran their budgets by 10% to 50%. The timeline is not a policy choice. It is a property of the industry.

For an investor, the practical conclusion is to treat any rare earth project that promises production in under five years with skepticism, and any project that promises it in under three with disbelief — and to value the companies that already produce, rather than the ones that promise to. For a policymaker, it is to recognize that the January 1, 2027 deadline for defense magnets will arrive before the supply chain is ready, and to plan for the gap rather than pretending it does not exist. And for anyone in the business of critical minerals, the missing number is now obvious. Nobody publishes a real-time tracker of rare earth project timelines — decision to commissioning, budget to actual, promised to delivered — by project, by country, over time. The firm that does will own the next argument in critical minerals.
