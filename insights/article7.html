Rare Earth ETFs vs. Mining Stocks: Which Captures the Price Spike?

rare earth etf remx mining stocks performance · November 2026 · 9 min read

On July 10, 2025, MP Materials rose about 51 per cent in a single trading session, from around US$30 to US$45.23, on the news that the US Department of Defense was buying a 15 per cent stake in the company. Volume hit 85.6 million shares, nearly fourteen times the daily average. Anyone holding MP Materials directly had the best day of the rare earth cycle. Anyone holding the rare earth ETF had a good day, and a much smaller one. That gap — between the stock and the basket that supposedly owns the theme — is the whole question.

The trade seems obvious in hindsight: buy the miners, not the fund. The numbers from 2025 back it up, and they are dramatic enough to deserve a closer look before anyone acts on them. Because the same figures that make the stocks look like the right answer also show why the answer changes with the year you pick.

What the ETF actually owns

Start with the fund most investors mean when they say "the rare earth ETF": VanEck's Rare Earth and Strategic Metals ETF, ticker REMX, listed on NYSE Arca since October 2010. As of October 31, 2026, the US fund held 37 stocks with US$1,448.12 million in net assets and a net expense ratio of 0.53 per cent. Its ten largest holdings made up 61.56 per cent of the fund. The two largest were not rare earth companies at all: Sociedad Química y Minera de Chile (SQM), a lithium producer, at 8.69 per cent, and Albemarle, also lithium, at 8.67 per cent. China Northern Rare Earth followed at 7.24 per cent, then MP Materials at 7.10 per cent and Lynas Rare Earths at 6.80 per cent. Only three of the ten largest holdings were rare earth producers.

The country weights tell the same story from a different angle. As of September 30, 2026, China accounted for 29.27 per cent of the fund, Australia 24.65 per cent and the United States 17.71 per cent. A fund bought as a bet on Western supply chains outside China holds nearly a third of its assets in Chinese companies — including the largest rare earth producer in the country. That is not a flaw in the fund's construction. REMX tracks the MVIS Global Rare Earth/Strategic Metals Index, which covers rare earths and strategic metals together, and lithium is a strategic metal. But it means the fund's returns answer a different question than the one most buyers think they are asking.

The 2025 scoreboard

In 2025, the question seemed settled. REMX returned 92.05 per cent for the year, according to VanEck's annual shareholder report dated December 31, 2025 — a superb year by any standard, and the fund's leading contributors were MP Materials, China Northern Rare Earth and Lynas. MP Materials itself did far better: its shares rose 223.8 per cent in 2025, according to S&P Global Market Intelligence data cited by the Motley Fool in February 2026, after surging more than 500 per cent by mid-October before giving some of it back. Lynas roughly doubled over the same year. The single stocks captured the spike; the basket captured a diluted version of it, dragged by lithium names and by holdings that had nothing to do with the export-control story.

The longer record cuts the other way. REMX lost 34.70 per cent in 2024. As of September 30, 2026, its net asset value had returned minus 0.79 per cent over one year, minus 0.02 per cent annualised over three years, minus 7.12 per cent annualised over five years, and 6.07 per cent annualised over ten years. A decade of holding the basket bought roughly the return of a savings account with far more volatility. The stocks were no steadier — Lynas fell 2.89 per cent in 2024 before its 2025 surge — but the comparison shows what diversification actually costs in a sector where three or four names drive the whole story: it costs the upside, and it does not reliably remove the downside.

The 2026 round trip

Then came the reversal that makes the 2025 scoreboard dangerous to read on its own. By November 2026 the spike had unwound hard. REMX was down about 38 per cent year to date as of November 18, 2026, trading near US$45 after touching a 52-week high of US$111.55 in May. MP Materials, which had reached a 52-week high of US$76.80 on May 8, 2026, traded at US$37.32 on November 19, 2026 — more than half its value gone in six months, with a market capitalisation of US$6.21 billion. Lynas fell from a 52-week high of A$22.37 in April 2026 to A$12.79 on November 19. The stocks that captured the spike also captured the fall, and they fell further than the basket: MP Materials' beta of 2.17, reported by Finnhub in November 2026, says the stock moves roughly twice as far as the market in either direction, and in 2026 it did.

The operating numbers explain why the market repriced both names so fast. MP Materials reported a loss per share of US$0.505 for fiscal 2025 and revenue of US$53.6 million with a loss of US$0.10 per share in the third quarter of fiscal 2025. Lynas reported net profit after tax of just A$8.0 million in fiscal 2025, down about 90 per cent year on year, on revenue of A$556.5 million — before recovering to A$80.2 million of net profit in the first half of fiscal 2026. These are strategic assets with government backing and genuine production, and they are also, by conventional measures, expensive and volatile businesses. The Pentagon's US$110-per-kilogram NdPr price floor underwrites MP Materials' revenue. It does not underwrite its share price.

The new pure play

The market has noticed the gap between REMX and the theme it claims to cover, and in April 2026 Sprott launched an answer: the Rare Earths Ex-China ETF, ticker REXC, on Nasdaq. The fund holds 41 companies, all outside China, charges 0.65 per cent a year, and held US$71.8 million in net assets as of October 13, 2026. Its concentration is the point: MP Materials was 20.44 per cent of the fund and Lynas 18.81 per cent on that date, together about 39 per cent of assets, and the top ten holdings made up roughly 76 per cent. Rare earth equities were 96.59 per cent of the fund at June 30, 2026. By November 18, 2026, Sprott's own holdings file showed Lynas at 23.14 per cent and MP Materials at 20.89 per cent — the two names now nearly 44 per cent of the fund between them.

REXC is, in effect, a two-stock bet with forty-one names attached. That is a fair description of the ex-China rare earth industry itself, which has exactly two scaled producers. An investor who buys REXC gets the pure play REMX never offered, and gives up the one thing REMX actually provided: distance from the fortunes of two companies. The fee is higher, the fund is far smaller, and its history runs to months, not years.

The basket caught the 2025 rally at half strength — and the 2026 fall at something close to full strength. That is the trade, stated plainly.

The counterargument: the case for the basket

The case for the ETF is not about returns. It is about ruin. Molycorp, the previous owner of Mountain Pass, rose from a US$14 IPO in July 2010 to US$79.16 in May 2011 and filed for bankruptcy in June 2015. The mine was bought out of bankruptcy for about US$20.5 million in 2017. A single-stock holder in 2011 lost nearly everything; a basket holder lost a bad year. Rare earth equities have a documented habit of going to zero, and the two scaled producers that dominate the ex-China story are also the two names whose execution risk is highest: MP Materials is building heavy rare earth separation it has never done at scale, and Lynas is commissioning plants that have already run A$300 million over budget at Kalgoorlie.

There is also the question of what the spike actually was. The 2025 rally was driven by Chinese export controls and by government contracts, not by a surge in magnet demand. If Beijing extends its suspension, or if prices normalise, the miners fall first and furthest — as 2026 showed. The ETF holder gives up the best months. The stock holder gives up the sleep. Neither is wrong; they are different trades, and the honest version of the debate admits that.

What to take from the comparison

The comparison resolves into a question about the thesis, not the vehicle. If the thesis is that rare earth prices spike on the next export-control headline, the single stocks have captured more of every spike so far — MP Materials at 223.8 per cent in 2025 against REMX at 92.05 per cent — and lost more of every reversal. If the thesis is that a Western supply chain gets built over a decade, the basket's diversification is worth more than the spike, and the newer REXC offers a middle ground: pure-play exposure with the concentration risk stated on the label rather than hidden inside a lithium-heavy index.

Three numbers should sit on any investor's desk before choosing. REMX charges 0.53 per cent, holds 37 names, and puts about 29 per cent of its assets in China. REXC charges 0.65 per cent, holds 41 names, and puts about 44 per cent of its assets in two companies. MP Materials and Lynas are the trade either way — the only decision is how much of them you want, and how much else you are willing to own to get them.
