How Much Does It Cost to Build a Rare Earth Mine Outside China?

mine development separation refining capex · November 2026 · 9 min read

In July 2026, the chief executive of Arafura Rare Earths stood before the WA Mining Club in Perth and gave the room a number that reframes the whole rare earth investment case. Darryl Cuzzubbo told the audience that at Nolans, his company's mine-to-oxide project 135 kilometres north of Alice Springs, about 90 per cent of the capital cost sits in infrastructure and the process plant. About 10 per cent sits in the mine. The hole in the ground, in other words, is the cheap part.

That split is the answer to the question every investor asks after a price spike: what does it actually cost to build a rare earth mine outside China? The honest answer is that nobody is really building a mine. They are building a chemical plant that happens to have a mine attached to it, and the chemical plant sets the budget, the timeline, and most of the risk. Get the mine wrong and you lose months. Get the separation plant wrong and you lose the project.

The bill, project by project

Start with Nolans, because it is the furthest advanced of the new Western builds and the only one with a positive final investment decision behind it. Arafura took that decision in May 2026, with construction targeted to start in September of the same year, subject to the last funding milestones. The company's June 2026 clarification to the ASX put total capital cost at US$1,191 million: US$998 million of pre-production capital, US$106 million of other pre-production costs and escalation, and US$87 million of contingency. For that money, Nolans is designed to produce 4,440 tonnes a year of neodymium-praseodymium oxide over a 38-year mine life, with first production expected from mid-2029 after a little over three years of construction and a two-year ramp-up. Divide one number by the other and the arithmetic is stark: roughly US$268,000 of capital for every tonne of annual NdPr capacity. That is derived arithmetic from Arafura's own figures, not a company quote, and it excludes the operating cost of US$46.40 per kilogram of NdPr that Arafura also disclosed.

Then there is the project with no mine at all. Iluka Resources is building Australia's first fully integrated rare earths refinery at Eneabba in Western Australia, processing stockpiled monazite rather than digging fresh ore. At 30 September 2026 the refinery was 65 per cent complete, with A$1,237 million of capital already spent against a total estimate of A$1.7 to A$1.8 billion, according to Iluka's September 2026 quarterly report. The refinery will produce separated magnet rare earth oxides — neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium and terbium — and it is being built in partnership with the Australian Government, backed by a non-recourse loan under the Critical Minerals Facility administered by Export Finance Australia. A plant that refines other people's ore costs nearly as much as a whole mine-to-oxide project. That tells you where the money goes.

Lynas Rare Earths learned the same lesson a different way: by paying for it twice. Its Kalgoorlie processing facility in Western Australia was first budgeted at A$500 million. By the time it opened in November 2024, the final cost had reached A$800 million — A$300 million over the original figure, after an August 2023 budget of A$730 million had already been revised upward. The plant cracks and leaches concentrate from the Mt Weld mine into a mixed rare earth carbonate, which is then shipped to Malaysia for separation into oxides. It does not even do the hardest step. Lynas also reported sulfuric acid supply problems at Kalgoorlie, a reminder that a separation plant is a chemical operation with chemical supply chains. The company still made it work: in the June 2026 quarter it reported 3,481 tonnes of total rare earth oxide production, including 1,857 tonnes of NdPr, on sales revenue of A$288.9 million.

The American version of the bill is being written at Mountain Pass, the only operating rare earth mine in the United States. MP Materials' July 10, 2025 agreement with the Department of Defense put the Pentagon into the capital stack directly: a US$400 million equity investment for roughly a 15 per cent stake, a US$150 million loan earmarked for heavy rare earth separation, a ten-year price floor of US$110 per kilogram for NdPr, and a ten-year offtake for magnets from a new 10X facility. An earlier Defense Department award of US$35 million had funded the design and construction of heavy rare earth separation capacity at the site. Five days after the Pentagon deal, Apple committed US$500 million to buy American-made magnets from the company. MP Materials was annualising near 4,000 tonnes of NdPr when the deal was struck, and the heavy separation the loan pays for is still being built. The mine has been running for years. The chemistry is the new part.

The smallest cheque in the sector may be the most instructive. Energy Fuels began construction in 2026 on a heavy rare earth expansion at its White Mesa Mill in Utah, at an estimated capital cost of approximately US$104 million. The expansion adds about 20 tonnes a year of terbium oxide and 120 tonnes a year of dysprosium oxide, alongside samarium, europium and gadolinium circuits, to a mill that already has commercial capacity for up to 1,000 tonnes a year of separated NdPr oxide. Commissioning of the terbium and dysprosium circuits is targeted for the end of 2027. A further, larger step is already on paper: a January 2026 bankable feasibility study for a Phase 2 expansion put capital cost at US$410 million for 6,000 tonnes a year of NdPr oxide, at an all-in production cost of US$29.39 per kilogram — a company study, not a built result, and it should be read as one.

Why the plant costs more than the pit

The reason is chemistry, not geology. The seventeen rare earth elements are chemically near-identical, and pulling them apart means solvent extraction: hundreds of mixer-settler stages, each nudging one element away from its neighbours, fed by reagents, power and water, and producing tailings that carry thorium and demand permanent management. A gold plant crushes rock and washes it. A rare earth plant runs a refinery. The ore body can be rich — Nolans holds reserves of 29.5 million tonnes grading 2.9 per cent total rare earth oxides — and still be the easy part of the job.

Location compounds the cost. Nolans sits in the Northern Territory desert, 135 kilometres from Alice Springs, which means roads, power, water and a construction workforce have to be built along with the plant. Permitting in Australia and the United States runs in years, not months, and a separation plant needs environmental approvals that a simple open pit does not. Every Western project in this article carries some form of government money — the Pentagon's US$550 million at MP Materials, Export Finance Australia's loan at Eneabba, up to A$200 million from the Northern Australia Infrastructure Facility for Nolans enabling infrastructure — because private capital alone has repeatedly refused to fund the chemistry at Chinese prices.

The mine is the press release. The separation plant is the budget — and the part most likely to run late, run over, or not work at all.

The counterargument: China builds it for less

Sceptics of the Western build-out have a strong case, and it starts with the last cycle. In 2010, China restricted rare earth exports and prices soared: lanthanum and neodymium jumped fivefold during 2011, and Molycorp, the owner of Mountain Pass, saw its shares climb from a US$14 IPO price in July 2010 to US$79.16 in May 2011. Then prices collapsed in late 2011, Molycorp filed for bankruptcy protection in June 2015, and MP Materials acquired the Mountain Pass assets out of bankruptcy for about US$20.5 million in 2017. The mine that cost roughly US$2 billion to revive was bought for the price of a suburban office block. Anyone underwriting a US$1.2 billion build on the back of 2026 prices should be able to say why this time is different.

There is also the question of whether the new economics are real economics at all. The US$110-per-kilogram NdPr floor the Pentagon set for MP Materials sits far above Chinese market rates, and the Iluka and Nolans projects both lean on government loans. If Beijing extends its export-control suspension, or if Chinese prices fall far enough for long enough, Western plants built on subsidised capital face the same arithmetic that bankrupted Molycorp. High capital cost does not guarantee high returns: Lynas reported net profit after tax of just A$8.0 million in fiscal 2025, down about 90 per cent year on year, while commissioning Kalgoorlie and absorbing its depreciation — though the recovery was sharp, with A$80.2 million of net profit in the first half of fiscal 2026.

But the counterargument has a limit, and it is the same one that applies to the price gap itself. The April 2025 Chinese controls on seven heavy rare earths were never suspended. The January 1, 2027 US defense ban on Chinese-origin rare earth magnets takes full effect in weeks. Buyers are no longer paying only for the element; they are paying for supply that does not require a ministry's permission. A separation plant that can deliver dysprosium without a licence is worth more than the same plant's output priced in Shanghai, and that premium is now written into government contracts, not just into spot quotes.

What the number means

For an investor, the practical conclusion is to underwrite the separator, not the miner. Ask three questions of any Western rare earth project: how much of the capital is in the process plant rather than the pit, who is funding it, and when does separated oxide actually ship. Nolans answers with a US$1,191 million budget and first production from mid-2029. Eneabba answers with A$1.7 to A$1.8 billion and commissioning in 2027. White Mesa answers with US$104 million and heavy oxide circuits at the end of 2027. The dates matter as much as the dollars, because a plant that ships in 2027 captures the premium while it lasts, and a plant that ships in 2029 has to survive whatever the market looks like then.

The cost of building a rare earth mine outside China is, in the end, the cost of building a chemical industry outside China. The rock is the smallest line on the invoice. The chemistry is the project — and the chemistry is where the money, the delays, and the returns will all be decided.
